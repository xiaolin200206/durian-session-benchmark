## Highlights

- PlantVillage: 40,490 images are only 7,524 leaves; image-level splits leak 99.6%
- 560 durian field images are 73 capture sessions; 96.7% of test images leak
- Session-level grouping lowers macro F1 by 12.2 pp and reorders nine architectures
- Hash and DINOv2 audits do not recover capture structure from released images
- Zero-shot transfer to a second region fails; one class correspondence is rejected

## Abstract

Accuracy figures for image-based classifiers depend on a partitioning decision whose relation to the collection process is rarely stated. We show that for field imagery the sampling unit, not the image count, determines what those figures mean, and that content-based methods cannot recover that unit from a released dataset. Over one annual cycle we collected 560 disease images from durian orchards in Peninsular Malaysia; they resolve into 73 capture sessions recovered from camera filename structure. Under an image-level split, 58 of 60 test images (96.7%) belong to a session appearing in training; the estimate moves only from 90.4% to 96.1% across a 2.6-fold change in that rule. The structure is not peculiar to our data: against PlantVillage's recorded leaf identifiers, 40,490 images resolve into 7,524 leaves, and an image-level split places 99.6% of test images in a leaf seen in training. Partitioning by session on identical data, seeds and protocol lowers macro F1 by 12.2 percentage points across all nine architectures (range 4.9–18.4) and alters their ranking; grouped cross-validation gives 59.6% against 79.3% for the image-level control, with folds spanning 41.9–72.0%. Two families of post hoc audit fail to recover the structure: a difference hash groups 0.3% of same-session pairs, self-supervised features reach no usable threshold, and the calibrated operating point collapses on a second dataset. Zero-shot transfer to that dataset loses 20.9–42.3 points against a 32.5% chance baseline, and one of four class correspondences is rejected. We release the dataset with capture-session identifiers and the audit tooling.

**Keywords:** Sampling design; Pseudoreplication; Data leakage; Field image data; Model transferability; *Durio zibethinus*

## 1. Introduction

Field images are not independent observations, and the structures that make them dependent are generated by the act of collecting them. A camera trap fires and records a burst of frames of one animal. An acoustic recorder captures one individual repeatedly at one station. A citizen-science observation carries several photographs of one plant. A scout photographs one lesion from three angles, films a branch, and forwards the batch. In every case a single episode of capture produces many images that share a subject, a background, an illumination condition and a sensor.

In the vocabulary of ecological and agricultural experimental design these are subsamples of one observation unit, and treating them as independent replicates is pseudoreplication (Hurlbert, 1984). When a dataset structured this way is partitioned at the image level, near-identical views of one subject land on both sides of the train–test boundary, and the model can score by re-recognising the subject instead of by recognising the phenomenon. The correction is established one scale up: in remote sensing and spatial modelling, random cross-validation over spatially autocorrelated samples inflates the apparent skill of convolutional networks (Kattenborn et al., 2022), and spatial cross-validation, though it reports lower cross-validated scores, yields models that transfer better to unseen fields (Stiller et al., 2024). Ecology formalised the general case for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical or phylogenetic structure (Roberts et al., 2017; Ploton et al., 2020), and leakage through non-independent records is a documented cause of irreproducibility across machine-learning-based science (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). Medical imaging arrived at the same practice independently, splitting by patient rather than by slice.

Grouping at the specimen scale is established practice in at least one influential plant image dataset (Mohanty et al., 2016, Sect. 2.1). What has not been measured, to our knowledge, is what its omission costs in field-collected imagery, where a session is harder to define than a leaf on a lightbox, and whether the structure can be reconstructed from a dataset released without it. Both questions are addressed here against a reference grouping recovered from filename structure, and both answers bear on how image datasets should be released.

We work with a perennial tropical crop, which supplies an unusually clean instance of the problem. Durian (*Durio zibethinus*) is among the highest-value tropical perennial crops in Southeast Asia, and the Malaysian sector has expanded substantially over the past decade. Official statistics put the national planted area at roughly 87,300 ha in 2022, up from about 70,000 ha earlier in the same decade (Department of Statistics Malaysia, 2023). Smallholdings remain the dominant farm structure, which is the feature relevant here: diagnosis is dispersed across many small operations rather than concentrated where an agronomist can reach it.

Pathogens and pests impose a substantial and unevenly distributed burden on global crop production (Savary et al., 2019). For durian, fungal, algal and oomycete pathogens are a persistent constraint under humid tropical conditions, and the disease complex in Malaysia includes algal leaf spot, anthracnose and other leaf blights, *Phomopsis* leaf spot, pink disease, and *Phytophthora* root and collar rot (Lim and Sangchote, 2003). Because a mature durian tree represents several years to a decade of establishment cost before first commercial bearing, the loss of a tree carries an economic consequence with no analogue in an annual crop system: the asset, not the season, is destroyed. The management bottleneck is not the intervention but the observation. Qualified diagnosticians are scarce in rural orchards, manual inspection does not scale across dispersed smallholdings (Sankaran et al., 2010; Martinelli et al., 2015), and early symptoms are easily missed under variable canopy light. Smartphone-based image classifiers are therefore promoted to growers and extension services as a substitute for the missing expertise, and they are promoted with a number: an accuracy, usually above 95%. A recent durian classifier, for example, is offered as a real-time diagnostic tool delivering near-instant feedback to farmers through a web application (Klangbunrueang et al., 2026).

This paper is about what that number measures, and about what a reader of a published dataset can and cannot verify for themselves. It makes four contributions.

**First, it bounds what post hoc auditing of a released dataset can establish.** Three content-based methods (a perceptual hash, ImageNet-supervised features and self-supervised DINOv2 features) are calibrated against capture sessions recovered from camera filename structure, on a dataset where that reference grouping is available. The hash recovers 0.3% of within-session image pairs; DINOv2 recovers substantially more but reaches no threshold at which precision and recall are simultaneously usable; and the operating point calibrated on our data produces a degenerate grouping, merging 90% of one class into a single cluster, when applied unchanged to a second dataset. The near-duplicate audit reported in Sect. 3.4 finds no contamination in a partition where 96.7% of test images share a specimen with training. The claim is bounded in three ways: to these representations, to single-linkage clustering, and to recovery rather than to any weaker notion of protective grouping. What generalises beyond those bounds is narrower and harder to escape: a publisher has no means of demonstrating, on a dataset released without identifiers, that any inferred grouping is correct.

**Second, it quantifies what capture-session pseudoreplication costs in field-collected image data.** Re-partitioning by session, on identical images, protocol and seeds, lowers macro F1 by 12.2 percentage points (pp) on average across nine architectures, positive for all nine (range 4.9–18.4), and grouped cross-validation exposes a fold-to-fold dispersion that the image-level protocol conceals behind a narrower interval. The inflation is not uniform across architectures, so leakage changes which model a practitioner, vendor or monitoring programme would select, not only the number printed beside it.

**Third, it reports what the resulting error rates mean in the field, and documents a failure of zero-shot transfer.** The errors are structured rather than diffuse: under session-grouped partitioning the dominant confusion is one specific disease pair, and the two classes that appear solved are the two supported by the fewest independent specimens, including the class whose misdiagnosis is the most expensive. Transfer to a second dataset from another production region fails, and one of the class correspondences between the two regional disease vocabularies does not hold; we give the two-inference-pass test that detects it.

**Fourth, it contributes a field-collected durian disease dataset** from Peninsular Malaysia (560 images in 73 capture sessions across five categories), released publicly with per-image capture-session identifiers, a documented collection protocol, the exact partitions used here, and the audit tooling used for the recoverability analysis, together with a reporting protocol for field image datasets.

## 2. Background

### 2.1. Image-based classification of field imagery and the field gap

Sensing at the individual-organism scale remains underdeveloped for perennial crops relative to arable cropping: a recent review found that yield-monitoring technology, standard equipment on a combine harvester, has no widely adopted equivalent for orchard and other horticultural crops, despite yield and health varying tree to tree (Longchamps et al., 2022). Disease diagnosis sits inside the same gap, and an image-based classifier is one of the few sensing modalities cheap enough to deploy at tree scale in a smallholding, which is why the validation protocol behind its reported accuracy matters.

Automated plant disease classification has advanced with deep learning. Mohanty et al. (2016) showed that accuracies above 99% were achievable on the PlantVillage dataset under controlled conditions. That study is also the closest precedent for the present one on the question of partitioning: its authors recorded which images depict the same physical leaf for 41,112 of the 54,306 images, and kept all images of a leaf on one side of every train–test split. Specimen-level grouping is therefore not new to plant image classification; what has not been done, to our knowledge, is to measure what it costs when it is omitted from field-collected imagery, or to test whether the grouping can be reconstructed once a dataset has been released without it. Those leaf identifiers are public, and Sect. 4.1 uses them to measure what an image-level split would leak on that dataset. Subsequent work replicated the headline accuracies across VGG (Alatawi et al., 2022), ResNet (Hu et al., 2020; Mandwariya and Jotwani, 2025) and EfficientNet architectures (Atila et al., 2021; Hanh et al., 2022), generally without stating whether the same grouping discipline was maintained. Such benchmark performance does not transfer reliably to field imagery. A systematic review of drone-based disease detection reached a similar conclusion from the sensing side: image quality, flight altitude and environmental variability at the point of capture, not model architecture, are the dominant sources of error in field deployment (Chin et al., 2023). Gauriau et al. (2023) documented a complexity–accuracy trade-off in real-world crop protection that benchmark evaluations fail to predict; Shoaib et al. (2023) identified the absence of field-robustness evaluation as a systemic gap; Fuentes et al. (2021) showed that tomato disease models trained on one farm generalise poorly to another; Kouw and Loog (2021) formalised the resulting dataset-shift problem.

For durian specifically, published studies have largely replicated the laboratory-accuracy paradigm. Daud et al. (2023) reported 99.1% accuracy on a small dataset whose collection sites and partitioning are not described; Al Gallenero and Villaverde (2023) evaluated CNNs on 1,344 images without field-conditions evaluation; Boondach and Chaisiriprasert (2024) incorporated colour-space enhancement for YOLOv8-based detection without evaluating cross-condition robustness. Most recently, Klangbunrueang et al. (2026) benchmarked CNN architectures on durian leaf disease images and deployed the result as a public web application. Across the durian studies we examined, none states how its partition relates to the capture process, whether repeated photographs of one specimen were kept together or split. We record what is absent from those reports rather than inferring what was done: a study that does not describe its grouping may still have grouped correctly, and the point is that a reader cannot tell. None of these studies evaluated transfer between production regions. Public field datasets for the crop have begun to appear: two Vietnamese durian image collections were released in 2025 (Thanh et al., 2025; Nguyen, 2025). Neither ships identifiers for the unit that generated the images, so neither supports grouped evaluation as distributed.

Attention modules have been a common route to incremental gains in this literature (Hu et al., 2018; Woo et al., 2018; Alirezazadeh et al., 2023). A negative result of the present study, reported in full in Supplementary Sect. S1, is that under leakage-free multi-seed evaluation no attention configuration separates from an attention-free baseline beyond run-to-run noise. We therefore make no architectural claim in this paper.

### 2.2. Sampling units and pseudoreplication in field image data

Ecological and agricultural experimental design distinguishes the observation unit from the measurement. Hurlbert (1984) named the error of treating repeated measurements of one unit as independent replicates *pseudoreplication*. It applies without modification to field image collection, where the hierarchy is

> **image ⊂ capture session ⊂ individual ⊂ site ⊂ region**

which in this study instantiates as

> **image ⊂ capture session ⊂ tree ⊂ orchard ⊂ production region**

and in camera-trap monitoring as frame ⊂ trigger burst ⊂ individual ⊂ station ⊂ landscape. An image is a measurement. A capture session, meaning one subject photographed in one visit as one burst of frames, is the smallest unit within which observations are dependent and between which they are approximately independent. An individual is the unit at which an ecological state or a management action applies. A site is the unit at which a tool is deployed or a recommendation issued. A region is the unit at which a model is licensed or a programme designed.

The scale at which a partition is grouped determines the question the reported accuracy answers. Grouping at the image level answers a question that is well posed but rarely the one intended: how well would this model classify another view of a specimen it has already seen? Grouping at the session level answers: how well would this model classify an *unseen capture session* at a familiar site? Because one tree may be photographed on two visits, an unseen session is not necessarily an unseen tree. Grouping at the site level answers: how well would it perform at a *different* site? Grouping at the region level answers: can this model be exported? These are different questions with, as we show, very different answers, and the literature reports the first while readers interpret it as the third.

Analogous corrections are established at the coarser scales. Ploton et al. (2020) demonstrated the cost of ignoring spatial structure in large-scale ecological mapping; Kattenborn et al. (2022) showed that spatially autocorrelated training and validation samples inflate performance assessment of convolutional networks; Stiller et al. (2024) showed for small-field yield prediction that spatial cross-validation reports lower scores than random cross-validation yet produces models that transfer better to external fields, the same asymmetry we observe here between validation optimism and deployment reality. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) survey the failure across machine-learning-based science and identify train–test contamination through non-independent records as its most frequent form.

What these literatures share is a generating unit that makes nearby observations dependent: a patient, a spatial block, a phylogenetic clade, a field. A capture session is that object at the individual-organism scale, and to our knowledge its cost has not been measured for field disease imagery. A methodological review of crop-yield modelling studies found that validation practice, not modelling technique, is the most common point of failure: data-splitting is routinely reported as if it demonstrated forecasting ability across sites or seasons when it does not (Filippi et al., 2025). Colaço et al. (2025) make the complementary point from the data-collection side, showing for on-farm experimentation that trial design and the spatial distribution of field data govern whether a dataset can support a machine-learning model at all. The present paper makes the equivalent case for field image classification: the failure is in what the partition is asked to demonstrate, not in the classifier.

### 2.3. Post hoc auditing of released datasets

Because grouping identifiers are rarely recorded, the practical question for a reader of a published dataset is whether the structure can be reconstructed from the images. A near-duplicate audit (exact hashing, perceptual hashing, or both) is a common element of dataset documentation, and a low duplicate count is sometimes offered as evidence that a partition is sound. Sect. 4.2 tests that inference against a reference grouping and finds it does not hold. Self-supervised representations are the natural stronger candidate, since models such as DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) are trained to produce features that distinguish instances rather than only categories, and we evaluate them on the same footing.

### 2.4. Regional transferability and technology transfer

Cross-dataset generalisation has attracted increasing attention since systematic evaluations showed that plant disease models trained on one dataset degrade substantially on others (Fuentes et al., 2021; Kouw and Loog, 2021). Wu et al. (2023) proposed multi-representation subdomain adaptation with uncertainty regularisation, showing that unsupervised domain adaptation can partially bridge the lab-to-field gap; Boorchoo et al. (2024) applied a DANN–CORAL hybrid for cross-dataset adaptation; Huang et al. (2025) found for UAV-based plant disease detection that transferring a modest fraction of target-domain data materially improved cross-site adaptability. The architectural and the data-centric results point the same way: a model developed at one site is not a model that works at another.

The practical question is whether a classifier developed in one production region can be deployed in another without local data collection: a technology-transfer question, not an algorithmic one. For tropical perennial crops we are aware of no cross-region evaluation in the durian disease literature. The present study additionally controls for preprocessing mismatch between source and target datasets, and treats the correspondence between the two regional label vocabularies as a hypothesis to be tested rather than an administrative preliminary.

## 3. Materials and methods

### 3.1. Study sites and image collection

Images were collected from commercial durian orchards across Peninsular Malaysia, including sites in Seremban (Negeri Sembilan) and Muar (Johor) visited directly by the author and additional orchards contributed by a collaborating grower partner, between July 2025 and June 2026, covering one full annual cycle including the main and off-season flushes.

Images were collected from approximately seven to nine commercial orchards; the exact orchard-level breakdown of images and sessions was not systematically logged at the time of collection, which is itself an instance of the missing sampling-unit metadata discussed in Sect. 3.4 and 5.1. Orchards varied in management practice, tree age and microclimate; per-orchard cultivar, size and fungicide programme were not recorded in a form that can be reported here.

Images were captured with an iPhone 16 Pro Max under ambient natural lighting, without background removal or standardised framing, deliberately reflecting the conditions a deployed system would encounter. The released files carry no camera make-and-model metadata (Sect. 3.4), so the device is recorded here from the collection record and not from the files; it is consistent with the released manifest, in which 526 of 560 files (93.9%) carry the same camera-generated filename prefix, the remainder having arrived through a grower's messaging app or being unmatched to a naming pattern (Sect. 3.3). The dataset therefore carries essentially a single sensor, and the consequence of that homogeneity for cross-device robustness is taken up in Sect. 5.6.

### 3.2. Disease categories and annotation

Five disease categories were established from field-observable symptom clusters consistent with the diagnostic vocabulary of local practitioners:

- **Algal leaf spot** (*Cephaleuros virescens*), foliar;
- **Leaf rot**, a field-observable symptom cluster dominated by anthracnose (*Colletotrichum* spp.) together with other necrotic leaf-blight symptoms that local practitioners treat with the same registered products, foliar;
- **Phomopsis leaf spot** (*Phomopsis* sp.; the genus is now largely treated under *Diaporthe*), foliar. The pathogen is also reported on fruit and stems of durian, but the images in this dataset are of foliar symptoms (spotted laminae and adjacent petioles);
- **Pink disease** (*Erythricium salmonicolor*, syn. *Corticium salmonicolor*), photographed on branches;
- **Root and collar rot**, attributed on field symptoms to *Phytophthora* spp. (*P. palmivora* is the pathogen usually reported on durian), photographed at the trunk base and collar.

The affected organ is stated for each class because the cross-region comparison of Sect. 4.6 depends on it.

Disease identification was based on field-observable symptoms assessed against practitioner and extension experience. No isolation, culture or molecular confirmation was performed, and the categories should be read as symptom classes and not as confirmed aetiologies. All images were reviewed and labelled by the author under the guidance of growers and extension staff with direct field experience in these orchards; ambiguous, dual-infection and low-quality images were excluded, yielding 560 images. The same collection also holds six pest-damage categories outside the disease vocabulary studied here (red spider mite, psyllid, leaf-hopper damage, weevil, stem borer and scale insect, 671 images in total). These were set aside before any analysis reported in this paper, and the five disease classes above, totalling 560 images, are the entirety of the released dataset. No second independent rater was available, so no inter-rater agreement statistic is reported (Sect. 5.6).

The dataset contains **no healthy category**. Every image depicts a symptomatic specimen, so the task studied here is conditional on disease being present: *given a symptomatic tree, which disease is it?* This is the task an advisor performs after a grower has flagged a tree; it is not the task a scouting tool performs when walking a block. The implications for deployment are taken up in Sect. 5.3.

### 3.3. The sampling hierarchy and recovery of capture sessions

We define the **capture session** as the unit of partitioning: one specimen, photographed in one episode, under one illumination condition (Fig. 1).

Sessions were recovered from filename structure. Frames decomposed from one video form one session; photographs with consecutive camera sequence numbers within a gap of three form one burst session; images received in a single messaging batch form one session; remaining files are singletons. Session identifiers are constructed within class, so a session cannot span two disease categories.

This resolves the 560 images into 73 sessions: 48 burst sessions (463 images), 7 video-derived sessions (63 images), 4 messaging-batch sessions (20 images) and 14 unmatched single files. Eleven of the recovered burst groups contain one image, so 25 of the 73 sessions are single-image sessions. The class-wise breakdown is given in Table 1 and the size distribution in Table 2 and Fig. 2.

This rule is a heuristic, not a ground truth, and it errs in both directions. Photographs of one tree taken on two visits, or with two devices, fall into two sessions and therefore still leak, so the recovered grouping is finer than the tree. Conversely, two adjacent trees photographed in one continuous camera sequence merge into one session, so it is coarser than the tree in other places. These two errors have opposite effects on a leakage estimate (under-merging deflates it, over-merging inflates it), and no argument from the rule alone establishes which dominates. We therefore do not describe the recovered grouping as conservative, and we do not treat the resulting figures as bounds. What can be established is how much the measurement moves when the rule is varied, and that is reported in Sect. 4.1 rather than argued from the rule's construction.

One property of the rule is not a matter of degree and we handle it explicitly. Sessions are constructed within class, so a continuous camera run whose frames were filed under two disease categories is divided into two sessions that can then be assigned to opposite sides of a partition. A single capture episode can perfectly well include more than one condition, more than one organ, or more than one specimen, and a class label is not evidence that the camera stopped. Applying the identical burst rule across classes instead of within them reduces the 73 sessions to **61 cross-class groups**, six of those groups each absorbing two or more published sessions; the largest holds 128 images spanning algal leaf spot, leaf rot and Phomopsis leaf spot. Sect. 4.1 reports the leakage measurement under this grouping alongside the others.

Within the published session-level partition, 41 camera sequence numbers appear under more than one class, and nine adjacent-numbered pairs (twelve images, 2.1% of the dataset) are split between training and test (Table S9). The cross-class grouping above shows the underlying dependence to be wider than those twelve images. Session-grouped partitioning therefore removes much, but demonstrably not all, of the dependence induced by the capture process, and independence between sessions is an approximation whose error we can bound in one direction only (Sect. 4.1, Sect. 5.6).

The provenance composition of the dataset is uneven in ways that bear on the per-class results. Of the 560 files, 526 (93.9%) carry the same camera-generated filename prefix, 20 arrived as a single messaging batch from the collaborating grower, and 14 carry other names. Two consequences follow. First, **all 63 video-derived frames belong to a single class**, Phomopsis leaf spot: together with that class's largest burst session of 78 images, one burst and seven short video clips account for 141 of its 157 images, or 89.8%. Video frames are the most redundant form of within-session imagery, so the apparent strength of this class (Table 8) rests on an unusually small number of filmed specimens. Second, six of the ten pink disease images arrived in the single grower-contributed batch, so the rarest class is also the class whose provenance is least like the rest of the dataset.

Across the whole dataset the median session holds 4 images, the largest holds 78, and the ten largest sessions hold 295 images — 52.7% of the dataset. The nominal sample size of 560 therefore rests on a strongly skewed and much smaller set of independent observations, and the concentration is class-specific: half of the Phomopsis images and nearly two-thirds of the root-and-collar-rot images come from a single session each. Pink disease (*n* = 10, three capture sessions) is a genuinely rare condition in the sampled orchards. A targeted inspection of several of the sampled orchards in the Muar region on 13 June 2026 found no active instances, and a crowdsourcing campaign through a grower network of approximately 2,000 practitioners yielded no additional images over the 12-month window. The class is retained because a deployed system must handle it, and its rarity is itself a property of the Malaysian durian disease landscape; its per-class metrics are reported for completeness but are not interpretable at this support.

### 3.4. Validation designs compared

Four grouping scales are relevant to this dataset (Sect. 2.2). Three are evaluated here.

**Session-level partition (primary).** An 80/10/10 partition made over whole sessions, giving 446/58/56 images (Table 1). The result was verified to contain no exact duplicates (MD5), no image carrying two class labels, and no cross-split pair within a perceptual-hash Hamming distance of 10. We report this check because it is the form of check commonly offered as evidence of a sound partition, and Sect. 4.2 shows what it does and does not establish. We further verified the grouping itself: no session appears in more than one of the three splits, so contamination between training and test under this partition is 0.0% by the session-level measure applied to the image-level control below.

**Image-level partition (control).** A stratified split of the same images at image level, the protocol prevalent in the literature, audited with the same tools. Two quantities should be distinguished, because they are easily conflated. Counting any boundary, 35 of the 73 sessions appear in more than one of train, validation and test, involving 446 images (79.6% of the dataset). The quantity that bears on a reported test score is narrower and larger: of the 60 image-level test images, 58 (**96.7%**) belong to a session that also appears in the training set, through 25 sessions common to both. The corresponding figures are 94.4% for validation against training and 70.0% for test against validation. Table S7 reports every pairwise overlap for both partitions. This partition divides 446/54/60 against 446/58/56 for the session-level one; the training-set size was matched deliberately, so that the two conditions differ in grouping rule and not in training-set size, at the cost of test sets of similar but not identical size. Each column of the paired comparison is what the corresponding protocol would report. This condition is retained solely as a controlled comparison (Sect. 4.4).

That the session-level training split contains 446 images and that boundary-straddling sessions under the image-level split also contain 446 images is a coincidence: the two quantities are drawn from different partitions and both equal 79.6% of 560 by chance.

**Grouped cross-validation.** Taking the capture session as the grouping unit, cross-validation uses StratifiedGroupKFold with session as the grouping variable. The fold count is bounded by the rarest class (pink disease, three sessions), so three-fold validation is the maximum that keeps every class present in each test fold. Within each fold an inner validation subset, also grouped by session, is held out for checkpoint selection, so each test fold is evaluated exactly once and never influences model selection. For tractability the cross-validation folds use a shortened schedule of 10 frozen-backbone and 5 fine-tuning epochs instead of the 15 + 15 of the main runs; the same schedule is applied to the grouped and the image-level condition alike, so the comparison between them is unaffected. The image-level control uses an identical protocol, including the inner validation split, differing only in that grouping is ignored, so the difference between the two conditions isolates the partition rule.

**Site-level (orchard) partition (not available).** Orchard identity was not recorded per image at the time of collection, and we were unable to reconstruct it from any information the released files carry. We audited the released files directly to establish this rather than assume it. Across all 1,120 released image files (560 full-resolution originals and their 560 downscaled copies), none retains a GPS tag, a capture timestamp, or a camera make-and-model tag; only 54 files retain an EXIF block of any kind, and those carry only layout fields left by the resize step (Table S8). The embedded metadata did not survive the repeated resizing and format conversion the files passed through during preparation. Device identity offers no discrimination for a further reason: the collection used a single handset (Sect. 3.1), so no device-based partition exists even in principle. Provenance type separates only the 20 grower-contributed images, which span four classes at supports of one to seven and cannot constitute an evaluation fold.

Leave-one-site-out validation, the design that answers the question a prospective user actually asks about behaviour at a *different* site, is therefore not available here, and this study measures three rungs of the sampling ladder (image, session, production region) but not the fourth. We regard this as a defect of our own sampling design and not a peripheral limitation. It costs nothing to avoid at collection time and cannot be repaired at any price afterwards, which is why it is the first item of the reporting protocol proposed in Sect. 5.5, and why Sect. 3.5 asks whether any post hoc procedure could substitute for it. The between-fold dispersion of the grouped cross-validation (Sect. 4.5), which ranges from 41.9% to 72.0% as different sets of sessions fall into the test fold, is the closest available indication of how much performance depends on which specimens a model happens to have seen; it is not a substitute for site-level grouping and we do not present it as one.

### 3.5. Testing whether capture structure is recoverable post hoc

For any already-published dataset, grouped evaluation is possible only if the grouping can be reconstructed from the released files. We know of no other public field disease collection that retains both a filename-derived grouping and the images, so this is the only place available to us where the reconstruction can be calibrated rather than assumed.

Three content-based groupings were evaluated, each applied by single-linkage clustering **within class**. Clustering within class is a concession to tractability and to comparability with the reference grouping, which is also constructed within class; it is not a claim that a capture episode cannot span two disease categories, which Sect. 3.3 shows it can. Single linkage was chosen because it is the rule under which two images join a cluster whenever any pair is close enough, which is the permissive choice for a leakage audit; its known weakness is chaining, and Sect. 5.6 states what that leaves unresolved. The three groupings are:

- **Difference hash (dHash).** 64-bit hash from an 8 × 9 luminance gradient; two images join a cluster when their Hamming distance is at most *t*, swept over *t* ∈ {2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12}. This is the family of method reported in most dataset-release audits, including the one in Sect. 3.4.
- **ImageNet-supervised features.** Global-pooled, L2-normalised EfficientNet-B0 features (Tan and Le, 2019) from ImageNet weights, with cosine similarity thresholded at τ.
- **Self-supervised features.** DINOv2 ViT-B/14 (Oquab et al., 2024), class token and mean of patch tokens reported separately, likewise cosine-thresholded. Self-supervised representations are the strongest available candidate because they are trained to separate instances rather than only categories.

All three use the evaluation preprocessing of Sect. 3.6 (resize 256, centre crop 224, ImageNet normalisation) and the 512 px working copies, not the full-resolution originals (Sect. 3.6). ImageNet and DINOv2 weights have not been exposed to these images; a model fine-tuned on them would cluster by what it memorised during training, which is the phenomenon under investigation, so no such checkpoint is used.

Agreement with the reference grouping is reported by pair counting over **within-class** image pairs: recall is the share of genuinely same-session pairs that a method places in one cluster, precision the share of same-cluster pairs that are genuinely same-session. Cross-class pairs are excluded because both groupings are constructed within class and would agree on them trivially. Precision must be read against the base rate, the share of within-class pairs that share a session, which for this dataset is **0.175**; a clustering that merges each class entirely scores recall 1.0 and precision equal to the base rate, and is not a recovery. Such collapsed solutions are flagged as degenerate and excluded from the choice of operating point.

We also report, on identical simulated image-level partitions (stratified 80/10/10, five seeds), the share of test images having a same-cluster sibling in training under the reference grouping and under each proxy. These two shares are reported side by side and are **not** divided into one another. A ratio of two aggregate rates is not a detection rate: a proxy that merges unrelated images can flag test images that the reference grouping does not, so the ratio can exceed one, as it does for the degenerate configurations in Table 3, and a value below one does not identify which test images were missed. Establishing what a content-based audit detects requires comparing the two flags image by image on the same partition, reporting how many leaked test images the proxy finds and how many it invents. That comparison is set out as an event-level contingency in Table S12, produced by `leak_detection.py` in the released code; we report it separately from the pair-counting statistics because the two answer different questions, and we do not summarise it as a single ratio anywhere in this paper.

Finally, the transferability of the calibrated operating point is tested by applying it unchanged to the external Vietnamese dataset of Thanh et al. (2025), where no reference grouping is available: the situation any reader auditing a published dataset is in.

### 3.6. Models and training

EfficientNet-B0 (Tan and Le, 2019), initialised from ImageNet weights, serves as the backbone. Its final feature map (1280 × 7 × 7 for 224 × 224 input) feeds a minimal attention module, a single 1 × 1 convolution producing a spatial score map that is normalised by a sigmoid and applied multiplicatively across channels before global average pooling. We refer to it as Lesion Focus Attention (LFA). LFA adds 1,281 parameters, 0.032% of the 4.01M backbone. It is reported here as one configuration among nine and not as a contribution; the ablation against no attention, SE (Hu et al., 2018) and CBAM (Woo et al., 2018) at the same position is given in Supplementary Sect. S1, and separates none of them beyond run-to-run noise.

Eight further architectures were compared under an identical protocol: EfficientNetV2-S, ResNet-50, ResNet-101, ConvNeXt-Tiny, VGG-16, MobileNetV2, MobileNetV3-Large (Howard et al., 2017, 2019) and ShuffleNetV2-1.0× (Ma et al., 2018). EfficientNetV2 has been applied to plant disease classification before (Devi et al., 2023).

Training follows a two-stage transfer protocol. Stage 1 (15 epochs) updates only the attention module and classifier head with Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) at a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³ over a frozen backbone; stage 2 (15 epochs) unfreezes all parameters and fine-tunes at 1 × 10⁻⁵. Adam is used with its default settings and no weight decay, and no learning-rate schedule is applied. The batch size is 16. The loss is cross-entropy with inverse-frequency class weights, combined with a weighted random sampler, to address class imbalance (Johnson and Khoshgoftaar, 2019).

Checkpoint selection retains the state with the highest inner-validation accuracy, evaluated after every epoch and carried across both stages, so stage 2 begins from the best stage-1 state and the saved model is the best-scoring epoch of the whole run. Selection is therefore on validation accuracy while the reported metric is macro F1. With the class imbalance of Table 1 the two criteria need not coincide, and selecting on macro F1 would have been the more consistent choice; we record the discrepancy here and return to it in Sect. 5.6.

Training augmentation comprises random resized cropping to 224 px (scale 0.7–1.0), horizontal flip (*p* = 0.5), vertical flip (*p* = 0.3), random affine transformation (rotation ± 20°, translation 0.05, scale 0.95–1.05), colour jitter (brightness 0.3, contrast 0.3, saturation 0.2, hue 0.05), Gaussian blur (kernel 3–7, σ 0.1–1.5), ImageNet normalisation and random erasing (*p* = 0.3, scale 0.02–0.10). Evaluation applies a resize to 256 px followed by a 224 px centre crop and the same normalisation, with no augmentation.

Main experiments were run on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 with PyTorch 2.8. Every configuration was trained with four random seeds (42, 1, 2, 3) in both partition conditions; results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over seeds, each table stating which, with per-seed values in Table S4.

The attention ablation and the architecture comparison were run as separate experiments, so the two shared configurations, EfficientNet-B0 with and without LFA, were each trained twice from independent random streams. We report both instances rather than reconciling them, and treat the difference between them as an estimate of run-to-run variation (Supplementary Sect. S1).

Images were downscaled to a maximum edge of 512 px with Lanczos resampling prior to training. The most aggressive augmentation crop retains 70% of the frame area, and the crop's aspect ratio is drawn from 3/4 to 4/3, so for a 512 × 384 frame the shortest side of the smallest admissible crop is about 320 px, above the 224 px network input; no upsampling occurs for any aspect ratio present in the dataset. Full-resolution originals are archived in the public release, but every figure reported here is computed on the 512 px copies, at evaluation as well as at training. This is not a formality: evaluating the same checkpoint on the full-resolution originals, through the identical resize-and-centre-crop pipeline, moves macro F1 on the held-out set from 72.0% to 77.6%, because reaching 224 px from 3024 px and from 512 px are different resampling paths and a 56-image test set is small enough for a few reassignments to matter. Anyone reproducing these numbers from the archived originals will not obtain them unless they downscale first.

### 3.7. Cross-region evaluation and testing the label correspondence

Primary evaluation uses macro-averaged precision, recall, F1 and accuracy on the session-level held-out test set (*n* = 56). Robustness evaluation applies five synthetic perturbations to that set: Gaussian noise (σ = 25), low brightness (0.4×), high brightness (1.6×), motion blur (radius 3 px) and 30% rectangular occlusion.

Cross-region evaluation uses the public Vietnamese durian dataset of Thanh et al. (2025), collected in January 2025 from Binh Phuoc and Tien Giang provinces, background-removed and resized to 400 × 400 px, with a shipped test split of 320 images over the classes used here. Four correspondences were defined onto the Malaysian vocabulary (Table 5).

Two qualifications belong with this mapping. The first is examined in Sect. 5.6 and the second tested in Sect. 4.7.

*Leaf_Blight.* The source dataset describes Leaf_Blight as symptoms arising from various or unidentified causes. Merging a residual category into a specific disease class introduces label heterogeneity on the target side, and the merge should be read as a treatment-equivalence grouping and not an aetiological one. We did not recompute the correspondence restricted to Leaf_Colletotrichum alone; a sensitivity check of this kind would use existing checkpoints and no retraining, and we flag it as unfinished rather than report a number we have not verified (Sect. 5.6).

*Leaf_Rhizoctonia.* This correspondence was made on treatment equivalence across different affected organs (the Malaysian class is photographed at the trunk and collar, the Vietnamese class is foliar), and we identified it in advance as the weakest link. Sect. 4.7 tests it and finds it does not hold. A foliar *Rhizoctonia* blight is itself reported on durian in the region (Lim and Sangchote, 2003); the correspondence fails, in other words, not because the condition is biologically absent from Malaysia but because it is absent from our five-class vocabulary, which is a sampling gap rather than a taxonomic one.

Models are evaluated zero-shot from Malaysian weights. Two averaging conventions must be stated, because both change the reported number without changing the model. First, macro F1 is averaged over an explicitly specified label set: classes absent from the target evaluation are excluded from the average, not entered as zeros. This is not the scikit-learn default, which averages over the union of true and predicted labels, so a model that predicts an absent class even once enlarges the denominator and deflates every score. All nine architectures predict pink disease on the Vietnamese data, assigning it 5.7% of predictions in aggregate (Table 13), so the default convention would divide by five instead of four and report values 20% lower than those given here. Second, source and target macros are averaged over the same classes: a five-class source macro minus a four-class target macro is not a transfer gap, and Sect. 4.6 reports both columns over matched subsets.

Chance baselines are reported beside every cross-region figure, since a macro F1 in the twenties or thirties is otherwise uninterpretable. For the four-class mapped task, with test-set composition (algal 70, leaf rot 126, Phomopsis 63, root and collar rot 61), macro F1 under uniform prediction is 24.4% and under prior-matched prediction 25.0%. For the three-class task excluding root and collar rot, the uniform baseline is 32.5%. These baselines describe a hypothetical classifier restricted to the evaluated label set. The models under test are not so restricted: they retain five outputs and can predict classes outside the evaluated set, which counts as an error without contributing to the average. A model that spends predictions on absent classes is therefore penalised relative to the stated baseline, and the comparison should be read as a reference point rather than as a matched null.

The external set was itself audited (Sect. 4.2 and Table S2). Its shipped partition is image level, which bounds the interpretation of the in-domain control below.

To establish whether the mapped task is learnable at all, an in-domain control was trained on the Vietnamese training split under the same four-class mapping, the same two-stage schedule and the same augmentation, and evaluated on the Vietnamese test split, with training images whose bytes also appear in the test split removed first.

To probe the preprocessing confound, a matched centre-crop condition (central 70% of the shorter edge, resized to 400 × 400) was applied to the Malaysian data and three lightweight architectures were retrained on it (single seed, on an RTX 4050 Laptop GPU under PyTorch 2.5.1 instead of the main configuration, and therefore interpreted qualitatively).

### 3.8. Decision-relevant error accounting

Macro F1 treats every misclassification as equivalent. Orchard management does not. We therefore report, alongside the aggregate scores, the structure of the errors and the direction of their management consequence.

Two consequences are distinguished. A **missed intervention** occurs when a condition requiring action is assigned to a class that local practice manages differently, so the appropriate action is not taken. An **unnecessary application** occurs when a condition requiring no action, or a different action, triggers a treatment. Their costs are asymmetric to a degree that depends on the class. For a foliar condition on a mature tree, either error costs approximately one application. For a trunk and collar condition, a missed intervention risks the tree and not the season, so the cost ratio between the two error types is far larger. We state this asymmetry qualitatively; quantifying it would require local agronomic and price data, and the classes here are symptom-defined rather than confirmed aetiologies (Sect. 3.2).

Three consequences follow for evaluation. First, the appropriate objective is cost-weighted, not macro-averaged. Macro F1 weights classes equally regardless of how many images each contains, and it does not distinguish the two error directions within a class at all, so it cannot express that one confusion costs an application and another costs a tree. Second, what a practitioner needs from an evaluation is the confusion structure, not the aggregate, because the aggregate cannot tell which of the two error types dominates. Third, the classes whose misdiagnosis is most expensive deserve the most independent sampling, and in this dataset they have the least (Table 2).

We do not attempt a costed recommendation. Local price data for fungicide rounds, labour and tree replacement in the sampled orchards were not collected, and a break-even analysis on assumed figures would add precision without adding information. What Sect. 4.8 establishes is the input such an analysis needs: that the error structure is concentrated rather than diffuse, that the dominant confusion changes when the partition rule changes, and that the model's out-of-domain failure mode is confident collapse onto a single class rather than uncertainty.
## 4. Results

### 4.1. The sampling structure of field image collection

The 560 images resolve into 73 capture sessions, so the nominal sample size exceeds the number of grouping units by a factor of 7.7 (Tables 1, 2). The structure is strongly skewed: median session size 4 images, maximum 78, and 52.7% of the dataset concentrated in the ten largest sessions (Fig. 2).

Under an image-level stratified partition of these images, 35 of the 73 sessions appear in more than one of train, validation and test, involving 446 images, **79.6% of the dataset**.

Because the grouping is reconstructed rather than recorded, the measurement must be shown to survive changes in the reconstruction rule. Over ten simulated stratified image-level partitions, the share of test images sharing a group with training is 95.2 ± 2.7% under the published rule (73 groups). Splitting more finely, by requiring strictly consecutive camera counters, yields 124 groups and 90.4 ± 2.7%. Merging across disease classes yields 61 groups and 95.4 ± 2.4%; relaxing the burst threshold to ten within class also yields 61 groups, at 95.4 ± 2.7%. Merging both across classes and at a threshold of ten yields 48 groups and 96.1 ± 2.0%. A 2.6-fold change in the number of groups therefore moves the estimate by 5.7 percentage points, all of it in the direction the construction predicts: finer grouping detects less leakage, coarser detects more. The measurement is a property of the data and not of the threshold, and the residual uncertainty it carries is small relative to the quantity being measured. The full sensitivity table is given as Table S14 and is reproduced from `sessions.csv` alone by `grouping_sensitivity.py`.

**The structure is not peculiar to this dataset.** Because PlantVillage ships the leaf identifiers its authors recorded (Mohanty et al., 2016), the same measurement can be made there against a grouping that was *recorded at collection* and not reconstructed, on two orders of magnitude more images. Matching the released identifiers to the image tree gives **40,490 images carrying a leaf identifier across 29 classes, resolving into 7,524 physical leaves**, 5.38 images per leaf. Under the same stratified image-level partition, **99.6 ± 0.2% of test images share a physical leaf with the training set** (ten seeds; per-class counts in Table S15, reproduced by `plantvillage_leafmap.py`). The identifiers do not cover the whole release: 29 of 38 classes and 40,490 of 54,306 images, against the 41,112 images Mohanty et al. report having mapped.

Two qualifications belong with that figure. It is a statement about what an image-level partition *would* leak on that dataset, not a claim that any particular study used one; Mohanty et al. grouped by leaf and did not incur it, and Sect. 2.1 records only that subsequent work generally does not state whether the same discipline was kept. And sixteen of the twenty-nine classes hold exactly four images per leaf, evenly enough to suggest transformed copies rather than separate photographs, which would make them duplicated files rather than capture-burst redundancy, the distinction Sect. 4.2 and Table 4 turn on. We tested it: over 2,126 within-leaf pairs drawn from one uniform class (*Potato* healthy, 4.00 images per leaf) and one non-uniform control (*Raspberry* healthy, 10.03), **no pair is one image under any of the eight dihedral transforms** at a mean-absolute-difference threshold of 2.0 on a 0–255 scale, and the two classes' difference distributions are indistinguishable (medians 29.65 and 28.15). The four images per leaf are four photographs. The check covers rotation and reflection but not cropping or rescaling, and we state it at that scope.

The comparison is the point rather than either number alone. A field dataset of 560 images and a laboratory dataset of 40,490 differ by a factor of seventy-two in nominal size and by a factor of 103 in the number of units that generated them, and both leave almost every test image sharing a specimen with training under the partition rule in prevailing use.

The figure that bears directly on a reported test score is narrower in definition and larger in value. Of the 60 images in the image-level test set, **58 (96.7%) belong to a capture session that also appears in the training set**, through 25 sessions common to both partitions. An image-level test set is therefore not contaminated at the margin: it is almost entirely composed of specimens the model has already seen from another angle. The equivalent figure is 94.4% for validation against training, so model selection is contaminated on the same scale as evaluation. Under the session-level partition the same measure is 0.0% (Sect. 3.4).

### 4.2. Two families of content-based audit do not recover the grouping

Table 3 and Fig. 3 give the calibration of the three content-based groupings against the filename-derived sessions.

**A near-duplicate audit recovers almost none of the grouping.** At its most permissive setting the difference hash places 0.3% of within-session image pairs in one cluster. On identical simulated image-level partitions it flags 4.6% of test images as leaked where the reference grouping flags 93.9%; we report both figures and not their ratio, for the reason given in Sect. 3.5. The mechanism is not incidental: two photographs of one lesion taken seconds apart differ in framing, angle and illumination, and a 64-bit global gradient hash treats them as unrelated, while a classifier can still recognise the specimen. The consequence applies to this paper as much as to others: the audit reported in Sect. 3.4 finds nothing to report in a partition where 96.7% of test images share a specimen with training. **Deduplication is not a weak form of grouping; it measures a different property.**

**Self-supervised features recover more, but not at a usable operating point.** DINOv2 class-token features trace a clean monotone trade-off, from recall 86.9% at precision 0.216 (τ = 0.80) to recall 0.6% at precision 0.951 (τ = 0.95), and the two methods coincide at the high-precision end: dHash at *t* = 12 gives recall 0.3% at precision 0.913, and DINOv2 at τ = 0.95 gives recall 0.6% at precision 0.951. The learned representation therefore extends the same curve towards higher recall rather than dominating the hash. No operating point recovers the grouping: the best pair-F1 attained by any configuration is 0.402 (DINOv2-cls, τ = 0.85), against 0.298 for the trivial grouping that assigns every image of a class to one cluster, and at that point 70% of same-cluster pairs are not same-session. *Recovery* is the criterion here: reproducing the reference grouping closely enough to stand in for it. A weaker criterion would be *protection*: a grouping too coarse to identify sessions could still reduce dependence across a partition, at the price of withholding more images from training. We have not evaluated the methods against that second criterion, and a method that fails the first may serve the second (Sect. 5.6). Mean-pooled patch tokens are weaker than the class token throughout, so the result does not rest on a pooling choice.

**The calibrated operating point does not transfer.** We selected τ = 0.86 on this dataset, the point where pair-F1 is at its plateau (0.399, against 0.402 at τ = 0.85) with the higher precision of the two, and on the shoulder and not the cliff of the recall curve. Applied unchanged to the Vietnamese dataset of Thanh et al. (2025), it produces a degenerate grouping: 414 Leaf_Algal images, 90% of that class, merge into one cluster, and 483 Leaf_Healthy images into another. Every threshold from 0.75 to 0.90 is degenerate by the same criterion, and only τ = 0.95 yields a non-collapsed grouping, reporting 16.5% leakage in the shipped split (Table 4). One mechanism is consistent with the data and we offer it as such rather than as a tested hypothesis: the Vietnamese images are background-removed and uniformly resized, which would compress within-class cosine similarity into a narrow high band, whereas raw field photographs spread it out. Fig. S5 gives the two distributions. Single linkage compounds whatever compression is present, because one close pair suffices to join two clusters, so the collapse should be attributed to the combination of representation, preprocessing and linkage rule rather than to any one of them (Sect. 5.6).

Normalising the threshold per dataset (by quantile of the similarity distribution, by singleton fraction, or by mean cluster size) would remove this problem by construction and is therefore not available: each of those quantities is a monotone function of how much redundancy is detected, which is what the audit is supposed to measure. Any such normalisation forces the datasets to agree on the quantity under test.

### 4.4. The partition rule changes every number, and the ranking

Table 7 and Fig. 4 pair the two partition rules on identical images, architectures, training protocol and seeds, so that only the grouping variable differs. Every architecture scores higher under the image-level split, by 12.2 pp on average and by as much as 18.4, and the difference is positive in all nine cases. We call this a protocol difference and not a leakage effect. The two conditions share the images, the architectures, the hyperparameters and the training-set size, so no difference in model quality is involved; but they differ in which specimens are trained on, which are used for checkpoint selection, and which are tested, and the test sets contain 60 and 56 images respectively. The grouping rule is the only thing varied, and everything that follows from varying it is contained in the 12.2 pp. Isolating the component attributable to a test image having a same-session neighbour in training would require holding the test images fixed while varying only whether their session-mates are available for training; we have not run that experiment, and we therefore do not report 12.2 pp as the measured price of leakage alone.

We deliberately refrain from attaching a *p* value to that consistency. Nine architectures trained on one dataset under one pair of partitions are not nine independent replicates of the leakage mechanism: they share the images, the sessions, the task and the protocol, so a sign or signed-rank test over them would treat as replicates precisely the kind of dependent observations this paper argues should not be treated as replicates. Applying such a test here would repeat, at the level of our own inference, the error we are describing. The descriptive statement is what the design supports, and it is strong enough without a *p* value. Quantifying the protocol effect inferentially would require resampling at the level of sessions, through repeated grouped splits, which the size of this dataset does not comfortably support.

Two properties of the inflation matter for practice.

First, **it does not track model capacity in these data**. Across the nine architectures, parameter count and the protocol difference show no clear relationship (Pearson *r* = 0.29, *p* = 0.45; with nine points this is consistent with a moderate association as well as with none, and is not evidence of independence), and the largest difference belongs to MobileNetV3-Large at 4.21M parameters, one of the smallest models in the comparison. Leakage is not a large-model problem that a practitioner can avoid by choosing a compact architecture for on-device deployment.

Second, **it does not preserve the ranking**. The inflation ranges from 4.9 pp for EfficientNetV2-S to 18.4 pp for MobileNetV3-Large, so leakage distorts the ordering between architectures and not only their absolute scores. The lightweight configuration used here stands fourth under the session-level protocol and last under the image-level one; ConvNeXt-Tiny, third under the session-level protocol, is first under the image-level one. This is the finding with the most direct consequence for a purchaser or a monitoring programme: the rank ordering of architectures changes between the two validation protocols, so model-selection conclusions can depend on the partition rule and not on model quality. We put it no more strongly than that, because several adjacent differences in either column are smaller than the seed-to-seed standard deviation of one of the two members. We state only what the table shows, that the two orderings correlate at Spearman ρ = 0.65, which with nine architectures falls just short of significance (*p* = 0.058), and leave the general question of whether leakage systematically reorders architectures to a study with more than nine points.

### 4.5. Grouped cross-validation, per-class behaviour and robustness

Grouped three-fold cross-validation averages 59.6 ± 4.5% macro F1 for EfficientNet-B0 + LFA over four seeds, against 79.3 ± 1.1% for the image-level control over the same four seeds under an identical inner-validation protocol, a difference of 19.7 points (per-seed folds in Table S5).

Three different quantities are easily conflated here and we separate them explicitly. The ± figures in every table of this paper are standard deviations across training seeds, and they measure initialisation and training variability alone. Fold-to-fold spread measures something else: how much the score moves when the *specimens* in the test set change. Neither is a confidence interval on deployment performance, and we do not present either as one.

The fold spread is the informative quantity, and under grouping it is large: 41.9% to 72.0%, a range of thirty points, reflecting that each test fold contains only 23–26 sessions, so which sessions land in the fold matters more than which seed was used. That is the honest indication of how much performance depends on the particular specimens a model happened to be tested on, and we report it rather than smoothing it away.

The seed-level ± figures should not be read as reliability. The image-level control is four times tighter on that measure (± 1.1 against ± 4.5), but tightness across seeds says nothing about sampling: when folds contain near-duplicates of their own training data, repeated runs agree closely with one another while all of them answer a different question. A small seed-level standard deviation is therefore not evidence that a figure is dependable, because it quantifies training randomness and not the choice of specimens.

The grouped mean also sits below the single held-out figure (76.1%), though the two are not strictly comparable: the folds use the shortened 10 + 5 schedule. Part of the difference is the schedule and part is that the held-out set samples one favourable configuration of sessions whereas grouped folds traverse all 73; the present design does not separate them.

Per-class behaviour changes qualitatively under session-grouped partitioning (Table 8, Fig. 5). Recall on algal leaf spot falls to 31.2% (F1 45.5%), with most errors flowing into leaf rot, a confusion the image-level protocol had concealed, because algal test images previously had same-session siblings in training.

Phomopsis leaf spot (96.6%) and root and collar rot (100%) remain strong, but Table 2 and the provenance analysis of Sect. 3.3 show why those figures should be read as provisional rather than as evidence of solved classes. Half the Phomopsis images and 62.8% of the root-and-collar-rot images come from one session each, and for Phomopsis a single burst plus seven short video clips supply 89.8% of the class. Under session-grouped partitioning the test specimens for these classes are not present in training, so these scores are not explained by specimen recognition. What they do rest on is a very small number of independent episodes on both sides: with one burst and seven clips supplying most of the Phomopsis images, the class is represented by few enough episodes that the score is sensitive to which of them fall in the test fold. These two classes are also the ones whose organ differs from the rest (branch and trunk against leaf), so a cue as coarse as background or bark texture could carry them, and a high score is not by itself evidence of disease recognition. Pink disease metrics are reported for completeness but are not interpretable at two test images from three sessions.

That class nonetheless carries a fifth of the weight of every five-class macro average in this paper, which is more influence than three capture sessions can support. We therefore report a sensitivity alongside the headline figure: excluding pink disease from the averaging set, and leaving training unchanged, the four-class macro F1 of the instance in Table 8 is 78.4% against the five-class value of 76.0%. Excluding a class identically from both arms of a contrast does not guarantee that the contrast is preserved, since the two arms need not perform equally on the excluded class; the four-class version of each comparison must be recomputed rather than inferred, and Table S13 reports it. A reader comparing our absolute numbers with another study's should also know which averaging set produced them. The grouped cross-validation is more exposed still: with exactly three pink sessions in the dataset, each test fold contains one, so a single specimen determines a fifth of that fold's macro score and contributes to the 41.9–72.0% fold range reported above.

### 4.6. Transfer to a second dataset in another production region

The primary cross-region result (Table 10, Fig. 6) covers the three foliar correspondences: algal leaf spot, leaf rot and Phomopsis leaf spot. Both columns are macro-averaged over those same three classes, so the drop is a like-for-like difference and not a subtraction between differently constituted averages. The spread of the drops is wide and, importantly for technology transfer, **it does not follow home performance**. Performing well at home does not predict transferring well, a relationship a single-region evaluation cannot expose, and one that undermines the common practice of selecting a model on local benchmark performance before deploying it elsewhere. Two qualifications belong with that figure. Part of the remaining gap is preprocessing rather than biology. The Vietnamese images are background-removed and uniformly resized; ours are not. A centre crop is not background removal, so this control tests sensitivity to framing and scale, not reproduction of the target preprocessing. Applying a matched centre-crop condition to the Malaysian data and retraining three lightweight architectures (Table S3) reduces the mean gap over the same three classes from 40.5 to 22.1 pp, a narrowing of roughly 45%. Because this control uses a single seed on different hardware and a different framework version (Sect. 3.7), we read it as a sensitivity analysis indicating that preprocessing mismatch is a substantial component of the gap, not as a precise decomposition of it. Most of that narrowing is the target side improving and not the source side degrading: Vietnamese scores rise by 14.7 pp on average while Malaysian scores fall by 3.7, so about four fifths of the effect is genuine recovery on the target and one fifth is the control discarding information at home.

The same preprocessing difference reappears in Sect. 4.2 as the reason a calibrated audit threshold does not transfer between the two datasets, which is an independent line of evidence that it is a first-order rather than a marginal factor.

### 4.7. One class correspondence does not hold

The fourth correspondence, Leaf_Rhizoctonia → root and collar rot, was flagged in Sect. 3.7 as the weakest: it was made on treatment equivalence across different affected organs, since the Malaysian class is photographed at the trunk and collar while the Vietnamese class is foliar. The data settle it.

Pooling the zero-shot confusion matrices over nine architectures and four seeds (Table 13), recall on the Vietnamese root-and-collar-rot images is 1.5%. The predicted class is not merely inaccurate, it is inactive: it is assigned to 1.1% of the 320 test images although it accounts for 19.1% of the labels, and every architecture agrees, with per-model recall between 0.0% and 8.2%.

That pattern is consistent with an organ mismatch: a model whose root-and-collar-rot representation is built from trunk and collar imagery has little to respond to in a dataset composed entirely of leaves. The in-domain control removes one alternative explanation, that the Vietnamese images are intrinsically hard or mislabelled: trained in domain, the same architecture fits this class at 98.4% recall (Table 12). The same 61 images are fitted almost perfectly by an in-domain model and almost never predicted by a Malaysian one (Fig. 7). This is consistent with an organ mismatch and it does not prove one, since a severe domain shift or a reliance on organ-level cues could produce the same pattern; the decisive evidence for rejecting this correspondence is the class definitions and the organ difference stated in Sect. 3.7, for which the confusion structure is corroboration rather than proof.

The three foliar correspondences behave differently, but not uniformly, reaching recalls of 84.2% (algal leaf spot), 43.3% (leaf rot) and 13.7% (Phomopsis leaf spot). The gradient tracks the strength of the basis on which each correspondence was made. Algal leaf spot, resting on agreement of both symptom and pathogen, transfers best. Leaf rot, resting on treatment equivalence and containing a residual source category (Sect. 3.7), is intermediate. Phomopsis leaf spot, resting on genus and symptom, is weakest, and at 13.7% it is closer to the failed correspondence than to the successful one. We do not claim this is a second mapping failure, since the class is organ-matched and its predicted share (10.5%) is not far below its label share (19.7%), so the output is active, not inactive. But the value is low enough that a cross-region deployment should not assume this correspondence holds without local checking.

We therefore report the three-class comparison as the primary result and retain the four-class version for continuity. Dropping the failed class does not flatter the outcome: excluding it raises the mean Vietnamese macro F1 across the nine architectures from 29.0% to 37.8%, but the chance baseline rises with it from 24.4% to 32.5%, so the margin over chance moves only from 4.6 to 5.3 pp. What changes is that the quantity being reported is one whose components all measure the same thing.

The wider lesson is one for programme design rather than for algorithms. Class correspondences in cross-region evaluation are an assumption requiring test, not a preliminary to be settled by naming, and a per-class confusion table on the target set costs two inference passes, with an in-domain control adding one training run. Any initiative that maps one country's or one advisory service's vocabulary onto another's (and any pooled multi-region dataset) needs this check before its aggregate score means anything.

### 4.8. The structure of the errors

The errors that survive session-grouped partitioning are concentrated, not diffuse, and their direction determines the management consequence.

On the session-level test set (Table 8), the dominant confusion is algal leaf spot assigned to leaf rot: recall on algal leaf spot is 31.2%, and eleven of the sixteen algal test images are assigned to leaf rot. This is a single-direction error between two foliar conditions that local practitioners do not manage identically, so its management consequence is a treatment decision taken on the wrong basis. We describe the direction of the error rather than cost it: establishing what each misdiagnosis would cost requires registered-product recommendations and local price data that this study did not collect. Under the image-level protocol this confusion is largely absent, because algal test images have same-session siblings in training; a practitioner reading an image-level evaluation would not learn that this particular confusion is the model's principal failure mode.

The two classes that appear solved, Phomopsis leaf spot and root and collar rot, are the two whose evidence is most concentrated in single sessions (Table 2, Sect. 3.3). Root and collar rot is the class whose misdiagnosis plausibly carries the highest cost, since trunk and collar conditions of this kind threaten the tree and not the season, and among the classes with interpretable support it is the one whose evidence is most concentrated: eight sessions in total, with 62.8% of its images from one of them. Pink disease has fewer sessions still (three), and is discussed separately because at two test images its metrics are not interpretable at all. Its perfect test score is therefore the least trustworthy number in the paper, and we flag it as such rather than reporting it as an achievement. The general point is that the sampling effort a class receives should follow the cost of getting it wrong, and in this dataset it does the opposite.

Under zero-shot transfer the error structure degenerates further (Table 13, Fig. 7). The models assign 49.8% of their predictions to algal leaf spot although it accounts for 21.9% of labels, and 1.1% to root and collar rot although it accounts for 19.1%. A model out of its domain does not distribute its errors evenly across the label set: it collapses onto one or two classes. Whether it does so with undiminished confidence we cannot say, because Table 13 records predicted labels and not predicted probabilities; a calibration analysis would be needed and we did not run one. For a decision-support deployment this is the important behaviour to design against, and it motivates the abstention mechanism discussed in Sect. 5.3.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1. The sampling unit for field image data, and why it must be recorded

The effective sample size of this dataset is closer to 73 capture sessions than to 560 images. That single fact explains the pattern of results: the inflation of Table 7, the depressed and dispersed grouped cross-validation scores, the gap between the held-out figure and the cross-validated mean, and the fragility of the per-class scores for the two classes whose evidence is concentrated in single sessions.

The session is a proxy for the true unit, and an imperfect one in both directions. For a perennial crop the natural observation unit is the tree, and the natural deployment unit is the orchard. Photographs of one tree on two visits fall into two sessions under our recovery rule and therefore still leak; conversely, frames from one continuous sequence filed under two disease classes are separated by a rule that operates within class, and twelve such images remain split across training and test (Sect. 3.3). The 12.2 pp we measure is what session-level grouping removes, and grouping at the tree would remove more. This is why we describe the 73 sessions as the finest recoverable grouping unit rather than as 73 independent observations: the claim the data support is that images within a session are certainly dependent, not that sessions are certainly independent.

The reporting implication generalises well beyond durian, and beyond agriculture. For any field image dataset, the number of capture sessions — and, better, of individuals, visits and sites — is the sample size that should be stated, and per-class session counts are more informative than per-class image counts. A dataset described as "5,000 field images" may contain fifty subjects or five thousand; the description does not distinguish these cases, and the difference is the whole of the dataset's evidential value. PlantVillage is the instructive case in both directions: its authors recorded the identifiers, which is why the measurement of Sect. 4.1 is possible there at all, and the figure that measurement returns, 40,490 images over 7,524 leaves, is not the one a reader takes from the image count alone. The same ambiguity attends a camera-trap collection described by its frame count, or a bioacoustic collection described by its clip count.

Sect. 4.2 is what gives that recommendation force. If capture structure could be reconstructed from the images, an unrecorded identifier would be an inconvenience and not a loss. The two families of method we tested do not reconstruct it: the audit conventionally reported flags 4.6% of test images where the reference grouping flags 93.9%, the strongest representation we tested offers no threshold at which its groupings are both complete and correct, and the threshold that performs best on our data collapses on another dataset. Our own reference grouping was itself recovered after collection (from camera filenames, which are source metadata that happened to survive, not from image content), and even that recovery is a heuristic whose error we can characterise only by varying it (Sect. 4.1).

Three things should be kept apart. Reconstruction from surviving source metadata, as we did, is often possible and is what the camera filenames allowed. Inference from image content alone is what failed here. Verification that either has succeeded is a third problem, and it is the one that does not have a cheap solution: demonstrating that an inferred grouping is correct requires a reference grouping, which is what was missing. A recorded identifier is not immune from error (it can be mistyped, and an identifier is not by itself proof of biological independence), but it can at least be inspected, and an inferred one on a released dataset cannot be checked at all.

Every other defect here could be reduced by more work: more seeds, more architectures, a better label mapping, a larger test set. The missing site identifier could be recovered only by returning to the orchards with records we did not keep, which for a year-long collection is not realistic. Sampling-unit metadata is usually inexpensive at the moment of collection and expensive or impossible afterwards, and it determines which questions a dataset will be able to answer.

### 5.2. What the transfer result establishes, and what it does not

The collapse from Malaysian to Vietnamese conditions survives session-grouped evaluation intact, and correcting the Malaysian baseline makes it more credible, since the drop is no longer computed from an inflated minuend.

Preprocessing mismatch is real but bounded. The matched centre-crop control narrows the gap by roughly 45%, most of it by improving the target rather than degrading the source. The residual after that control, about 22 pp, is the substantive finding, and two facts constrain how it should be read. First, the models are not at chance: seven of nine sit above the 32.5% baseline of the three-class task. Something transfers, enough to beat guessing but nowhere near enough to be useful. Second, the mapped task is not the obstacle, since it is learnable in domain to 93.0 ± 1.4%.

The defensible claim is therefore this. A durian disease classifier trained in Peninsular Malaysia does not usefully transfer zero-shot to this Vietnamese dataset, losing 20.9 to 42.3 macro-F1 points across the foliar classes and finishing between 4 pp below and 16 pp above the 32.5% chance baseline; no architecture among nine changes that, though they differ by a factor of two in how much they lose, and home performance does not predict which lose least; the target task is not the obstacle; and a single-seed preprocessing control narrows the gap by roughly 45%, leaving a residual of about 22 pp that is plausibly attributable to distribution shift. What remains is distribution shift in the broad sense: pathogen strain and symptom stage, cultivar and canopy, collection protocol and annotation convention. This design bounds that residual but does not decompose it.

One qualification follows from Sect. 4.2. The in-domain control's 93.0% rests on a dataset whose own partition is image level, and we cannot describe the resulting optimism as small merely because a hash audit found little, since on the one dataset where the comparison can be made that audit flags 4.6% of test images against 93.9% under the reference grouping. The 93.0% should therefore be read as an upper bound whose tightness is unknown. It remains sufficient for the argument it is used for, establishing that the mapped task is learnable, because even a substantially deflated figure would remain far above the 24.4% chance baseline and far above the zero-shot scores.

For a monitoring or extension programme the operational reading is straightforward: region-specific validation is not optional but a prerequisite, and a classifier acquired from another production region should be treated as an untested product until it has been evaluated on local data. Unsupervised domain adaptation (Wu et al., 2023) with modest target-domain data is the natural next step (Huang et al., 2025, found that transferring a fraction of target-domain data materially improved cross-site adaptability for UAV-based disease detection), and a few-shot curve over target-region capture sessions would quantify how little local data is needed. That curve, rather than a zero-shot score, is the number a programme deciding whether to import or rebuild a model actually needs.

### 5.3. Consequences for users, advisors and vendors

Four implications follow for anyone specifying or buying an image-based classifier for field use.

**The quoted accuracy answers the wrong question unless the grouping scale is stated.** A vendor figure of 97% obtained under image-level partitioning, and a figure of 82% obtained under session-level partitioning, can describe the same model on the same data (Table 7). A purchaser cannot distinguish them from the number alone. The single question to ask is: *at what unit was your test set separated from your training set?* If the answer is "randomly, by image", the quoted figure is an upper bound of unknown tightness. If the answer is "by site", it is close to the number that matters. A supporting answer of the form "we checked for duplicates" does not substitute, for the reason given in Sect. 4.2.

**Model selection is affected, not only model description.** Because inflation ranges from 4.9 to 18.4 pp and is uncorrelated with parameter count, a comparative evaluation run under the prevailing protocol can rank a worse model first. In our data the model that ranks first under image-level evaluation ranks third under session-level, and the model that ranks last under image-level ranks fourth. Procurement decisions and recommendations built on published comparisons therefore inherit the protocol error, not merely an optimistic number.

**The system as evaluated here is not a scouting tool and should not be sold as one.** Our dataset contains no healthy category, so the model answers "which disease is this?" and not "is this tree diseased?". A deployable scouting system needs three things this study does not provide: a healthy class; an abstention mechanism, so that low-confidence cases are referred to an agronomist rather than assigned a label; and open-set handling for conditions absent from training. Table 13 shows why the second matters most. Out of its domain, the model does not spread its errors or hesitate. It assigns half of all predictions to one class and effectively never predicts another. A diagnostic that fails loudly is more dangerous in the field than one that fails quietly, because a confident wrong label triggers an action.

**The objective should be cost-weighted, and the sampling effort should follow the same weights.** Macro F1 weights classes equally and does not distinguish error directions, but in this system the dominant foliar confusion costs an application while a missed trunk and collar condition plausibly costs the tree (Sect. 3.8, 4.8). The same weighting should govern data collection, and in this dataset it did not: the class with the highest error cost is among the most thinly sampled.

### 5.4. Five protocol choices that moved the numbers without touching a model

Five decisions in this study changed the reported figures without changing any architecture, loss, optimiser or hyperparameter, and none of them is exotic. Two of the five require retraining and therefore produce different weights; what is held fixed is the specification of the model, not the fitted parameters.

The **partition rule** shifted macro F1 by 12.2 pp on average and reordered the architectures. The **averaging convention**, a single missing argument that admitted an absent class into the denominator, deflated every cross-region score by 20%. The **class correspondence**, taken on trust, contributed a class scoring 2.8% F1 to an average presented as a measure of geographic transfer. The **evaluation resolution**, which is easily treated as a storage decision rather than an experimental one, moves the held-out score by 5.6 pp depending on whether the same checkpoint sees the 512 px copies it was trained on or the originals they came from (Sect. 3.6). And the **audit method** determines whether the dataset appears to leak 4.6% or 93.9% of its test set — the same images, the same partition, two grouping rules.

All five are invisible in a results table. We report them together because the pattern is the point: a reported accuracy is a joint property of a model and a set of protocol decisions, and the decisions are rarely written down.

### 5.5. A reporting protocol for field image datasets

We propose the following. Most of it is inexpensive at collection time and hard or impossible to supply afterwards.

- **Record and release the sampling-unit identifiers.** At minimum: capture session. Preferably also individual, site and visit. Every item below depends on this one; Sect. 4.2 shows that the content-based methods in common use do not reconstruct it after the fact, and it is the item we ourselves failed to complete at the site level.
- **Report the number of independent units alongside the image count**, per class as well as in total, together with the size distribution and the share of each class held in its largest unit.
- **Group the partition at the coarsest unit the intended deployment must generalise across.** For a tool to be used at a new site, that unit is the site, not the image.
- **Do not present a duplicate or near-duplicate audit as evidence that a partition is sound.** It tests a different property. Where no identifiers exist, report a content-based grouping with its representation, threshold, linkage rule and degeneracy check, and state that the resulting figure is uncalibrated: a proxy that under-merges will miss leakage and one that over-merges will invent it, and without a reference grouping neither error can be quantified on that dataset.
- **Report the chance baseline** for the exact label set evaluated, and state which label set the macro average covers.
- **Treat a cross-dataset or cross-region label mapping as a hypothesis.** A per-class confusion table on the target set costs two inference passes; an in-domain control additionally requires training one model on the target data. Neither is conclusive on its own, and both are cheaper than discovering the problem after publication.
- **Evaluate at the resolution the model was trained at**, and state it.
- **Use multiple seeds and report the spread between replicate runs of one configuration**, not only between conditions. A difference between methods that is smaller than the difference between two runs of one method is not a result.
- **State the error structure, not only the aggregate**, and identify which confusions carry which consequence.

### 5.6. Limitations

**Labelling and dataset scale.** All labels were assigned by the author under grower and extension guidance, with no second independent rater and therefore no inter-rater agreement statistic. Disease identification rests on field-observable symptoms without isolation, culture or molecular confirmation, so the categories are symptom classes rather than confirmed aetiologies. The dataset is dominated by a single camera family (93.9% of files share one camera-generated filename prefix), so robustness to cross-device variation is not measured; the synthetic perturbations of Sect. 4.5 stand in for that variation but do not quantify it. Provenance is unevenly distributed across classes: all video-derived frames belong to one class, and the majority of the rarest class arrived in a single grower-contributed batch (Sect. 3.3). Pink disease is represented by three sessions across the entire collection period; its metrics are not interpretable, and two further classes have their evidence concentrated in single sessions (Table 2), so their high per-class scores are provisional.

**Sampling design and the reference grouping.** The capture sessions are reconstructed from filename structure, not recorded at collection, and they are a heuristic. They are finer than the tree where one tree was photographed on two visits and coarser than the tree where two trees fall in one camera run, and the two errors push a leakage estimate in opposite directions. We have not verified individual groups against field records, which do not exist at that resolution; the strongest statement the design supports is that the leakage measurement moves by 5.7 percentage points across a 2.6-fold change in the number of groups (Sect. 4.1), not that any particular group corresponds to one specimen. Because sessions are constructed within class, a continuous camera run filed under two diseases is divided; applying the rule across classes reduces 73 groups to 61, so the session-level scores are optimistic relative to a partition grouped at the capture episode irrespective of class, by an amount we have measured for the leakage statistic but not for the trained models. Site identifiers were not recorded per image and cannot be recovered from the released files (Sect. 3.4), so site-level grouping and leave-one-site-out validation are impossible, and the study measures the image, session and production-region rungs of the ladder but not the site rung, the scale most relevant to a deployment decision.

**The recoverability analysis.** Three representations were tested under one clustering rule, and the result is a negative one on that finite set. Single linkage joins two clusters whenever any pair of their members is close enough, which is permissive for detection and prone to chaining; the degenerate groupings on the external dataset are consistent with chaining as well as with a compressed similarity distribution, and we did not evaluate complete or average linkage, density-based clustering, local feature matching, or a representation trained for instance-level copy detection. Nor did we define usability against a specific downstream goal: a grouping too coarse to identify sessions could still reduce dependence in a partition, at the cost of discarding training data, and we did not measure that trade-off. The claim is correspondingly narrow: these methods, under this clustering rule, do not reconstruct the reference grouping, and a publisher has no means of demonstrating on their own released data that another method would. The calibration rests on one dataset, because it requires a collection where capture structure survives in filenames and the images are public, and we know of no second such collection.

**Training and evaluation protocol.** Checkpoint selection uses inner-validation accuracy while the reported metric is macro F1 (Sect. 3.6); under class imbalance these criteria need not agree, and selecting on macro F1 would have been the more consistent choice given that training already reweights toward minority classes. Training combines inverse-frequency class weighting with a weighted random sampler, which rebalances twice; for a class represented by three capture sessions this means heavy repetition of a few specimens, and we did not run a sensitivity analysis separating the two mechanisms. The centre-crop control was run with a single seed on different hardware and a different framework version from the main runs, and is interpreted qualitatively. Results are reported at the 512 px working resolution; we have not established how they would change at other resolutions, only that they do change. The ablation and comparison experiments trained shared configurations independently, so two instances of the same model appear in the paper; we report both rather than selecting one, but a single unified experiment would be preferable.

**Cross-region evaluation.** Cross-region performance is weak in absolute terms, with seven of nine architectures clearing the chance baseline but none by enough to be useful, so the design bounds transferability without decomposing what limits it. The in-domain control rules out an unlearnable target but was run on a dataset whose own partition is image level, so 93.0% is an upper bound (Sect. 5.2). One of the four correspondences was found not to hold, one target class merges a residual source category and was not tested with that category excluded, and a third transfers at a recall low enough to warrant local checking (Sect. 3.7, 4.7). No costed decision analysis is offered, since local price data were not collected. Deployment on edge hardware, including runtime optimisation, is deliberately out of scope.

## 6. Conclusion

For image-based classification of field imagery, the sampling unit determines what a reported accuracy means. Five hundred and sixty field images of durian disease in Peninsular Malaysia resolve into only 73 reconstructed capture sessions, and under an image-level split 96.7% of the test images belong to a session that also appears in training, and macro F1 is 12.2 pp higher on average, by as much as 18.4, with the rank ordering of architectures altered. Session-level partitioning and grouped cross-validation (59.6 ± 4.5%, against 79.3 ± 1.1% for the image-level control) give the defensible figures, and the image-level control is tighter as well as higher, so a narrow interval is not evidence of reliability. The structure is not an artefact of one small collection: PlantVillage's 40,490 identified images resolve into 7,524 physical leaves, and the same partition rule places 99.6 ± 0.2% of its test images in a leaf that also appears in training.

That measurement is robust to the reconstruction rule, moving from 90.4% to 96.1% across a 2.6-fold change in the number of groups, but the structure itself is not recoverable from image content by the methods we tested. A near-duplicate audit of the kind reported for this dataset flags 4.6% of test images where the reference grouping flags 93.9%; self-supervised features recover more but offer no threshold at which their groupings are both complete and correct, the best pair-F1 of any configuration being 0.402 against 0.298 for merging each class entirely; and the operating point calibrated on our data collapses into a degenerate grouping when applied to a second dataset whose preprocessing differs. We do not claim that no method could succeed. We claim that these do not, and that a publisher cannot demonstrate on their own released data that another has, because demonstrating it requires the reference grouping whose absence created the problem.

Transfer between production regions fails: all nine architectures drop by 20.9 to 42.3 pp on Vietnamese data, leaving seven of nine above the 32.5% chance baseline and none near a usable level; a single-seed matched-crop analysis narrows that by roughly 45%, and an in-domain control at 93.0 ± 1.4% shows the target to be learnable under its own protocol. One of the four class correspondences between the two regional disease vocabularies does not hold, and we report the per-class evidence bearing on it, because a cross-region label mapping is a hypothesis and an aggregate score cannot test one.

The errors that survive session-grouped evaluation are concentrated rather than diffuse, and the class whose misdiagnosis is plausibly most expensive, root and collar rot, where the condition threatens the tree rather than the season, is among those our sampling supports most thinly: eight capture episodes, most of its images from one. That is a sampling problem before it is a modelling one, which is the argument of this paper in miniature.

We release the dataset with capture-session identifiers, together with the audit tooling, and propose that sampling units, not image counts, be recorded and reported as the sample size of field image datasets. Region-specific validation is a prerequisite rather than a refinement, and quantifying how much local data is needed to close the residual gap, through a few-shot curve over target-region capture sessions, is the immediate next step.
