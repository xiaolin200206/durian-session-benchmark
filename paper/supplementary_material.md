## S1. The attention module does not separate from an attention-free baseline

This section reports the module ablation that motivated the original design of the lightweight configuration used in the main text. It is placed here rather than in the main text because its result is negative and the paper makes no architectural claim.

EfficientNet-B0 (Tan and Le, 2019) is used as the backbone. Its final feature map (1280 × 7 × 7 for 224 × 224 input) feeds a single 1 × 1 convolution producing a spatial score map that is normalised by a sigmoid and applied multiplicatively across channels before global average pooling. We refer to it as Lesion Focus Attention (LFA). LFA adds 1,281 parameters, 0.032% of the 4.01M backbone. Three variants — no attention, squeeze-and-excitation (Hu et al., 2018) and CBAM (Woo et al., 2018) at the same position — share backbone, head, loss, optimiser and hyperparameters.

Four configurations were trained under the session-level partition at each of the four seeds (42, 1, 2, 3), one run per configuration per seed (Table S6). The means are:

| Configuration | Macro F1 (%), session-level partition, four seeds |
| --- | --- |
| EfficientNet-B0 + LFA | 79.1 ± 2.2 |
| EfficientNet-B0, no attention | 77.0 ± 4.0 |
| EfficientNet-B0 + CBAM | 76.5 ± 3.8 |
| EfficientNet-B0 + SE | 72.1 ± 2.3 |

The contrast the design question turns on — LFA against the attention-free baseline — is **2.1 pp with a 95% confidence interval from −7.4 to 11.6**. The interval contains zero by a wide margin in both directions.

**The informative comparison is between independent runs of the same configuration.** Because the ablation and the architecture comparison were run as separate experiments (Sect. 3.6 of the main text), LFA and the attention-free baseline were each trained twice, from independent random streams, at every seed. Those matched-seed pairs differ by **5.1 pp on average and by as much as 8.5 pp**.

> **Training the same model twice moves the score further than adding LFA to the baseline does.**

We therefore retain LFA as one option among alternatives this design cannot separate, not as a claimed improvement. It is not the smallest-footprint option either: the attention-free baseline has 1,281 fewer parameters, and the design cannot distinguish the two on accuracy.

Robustness under the five synthetic perturbations (Table 9 of the main text, plotted in Fig. S2) shows no consistent attention advantage either: the difference between the LFA model and the attention-free baseline ranges from +8.5 pp under high brightness to −4.1 pp under occlusion, with no ordering that holds across conditions.

Grad-CAM maps (Selvaraju et al., 2017) for the two configurations are given in Fig. S3. They are included for completeness and are not offered as evidence for the module: with the accuracy contrast smaller than run-to-run variation, a visual difference between saliency maps cannot establish that the module is doing what its name says.

## S10. Statistics for the ablation, and a retracted earlier finding

### S10.1 Tests reported for descriptive purposes only

A one-way ANOVA across the four attention configurations and pairwise McNemar tests between them are reported in Table S6b. **These tests treat the 56 test images as independent observations, which the argument of the main paper says they are not:** the test set contains 56 images drawn from a smaller number of capture sessions, so the effective degrees of freedom are lower than the tests assume and every *p* value below is anticonservative. They are reported as descriptive summaries of the observed differences, not as calibrated inference, and no conclusion in this paper rests on them.

*[Table S6b to be inserted from the author's `aggregate.py` output: ANOVA F, df, p; pairwise McNemar statistics and p values for the six configuration pairs.]*

### S10.2 A finding removed by a fourth seed

An earlier three-seed version of this analysis appeared to show that the ranking of attention modules reversed between the image-level and the session-level protocol. Adding a fourth seed removed the effect: the apparent reversal lay within run-to-run variation, and the four-seed values in Table S6 show no such reversal.

We record this because it is an instance of the paper's own argument applied to itself. With three seeds and a 56-image test set, a difference of a few points is indistinguishable from noise, and the reversal was an artefact of exactly the kind that a protocol without replicate runs of one configuration cannot detect. **Readers should not cite the reversal.** It is reported here only so that anyone comparing an earlier preprint version against this one can see what changed and why.

## Table S14. Sensitivity of the leakage measurement to the grouping rule

Reconstructed capture sessions are a heuristic (Sect. 3.3 of the main text). The rule is therefore varied in both directions — finer and coarser — and the leakage measurement recomputed under each. Leakage is the share of test images whose group also appears in training, averaged over ten simulated stratified image-level partitions (80/10/10, seeds 0–9), reported as mean ± s.d. across seeds.

Reproduced from `sessions.csv` alone by `grouping_sensitivity.py --sessions sessions.csv --seeds 10`; the images are not required, because the rule operates on filenames.

| Rule | Burst gap | Cross-class | Groups | Images per group | Largest group | Leakage (%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conservative split (strictly consecutive counters) | 1 | no | 124 | 4.52 | 39 | 90.4 ± 2.7 |
| **As published** | **3** | **no** | **73** | **7.67** | **78** | **95.2 ± 2.7** |
| Cross-class protective | 3 | yes | 61 | 9.18 | 128 | 95.4 ± 2.4 |
| Wider burst | 10 | no | 61 | 9.18 | 89 | 95.4 ± 2.7 |
| Conservative merge | 10 | yes | 48 | 11.67 | 156 | 96.1 ± 2.0 |

**Reading.** Across a **2.6-fold** change in the number of groups (124 → 48) the measurement moves by **5.7 pp** (90.4% → 96.1%), and the movement is in the direction the construction predicts throughout: finer grouping detects less leakage, coarser detects more. The measurement is therefore a property of the data rather than of the threshold. It is not a bound: the two errors of the reconstruction rule push a leakage estimate in opposite directions, and nothing in the rule's construction establishes which dominates (Sect. 3.3).

**Cross-class merging.** Applying the identical burst rule across disease classes rather than within them joins **6** groups of published sessions, reducing 73 sessions to 61. The largest resulting cross-class group holds **128 images spanning three classes** (algal leaf spot, leaf rot and Phomopsis leaf spot). This is a substantially wider dependence than the twelve straddling images reported in Sect. 3.3.

## Sect. S16. Transform-duplication check on the PlantVillage leaf groups

Sixteen of the twenty-nine covered classes hold **exactly four images per leaf** — mean 4.00,
maximum 4, no exceptions — covering 17,933 images, 44.3% of the covered subset. Photography does
not usually come out that even, and the obvious alternative is that the four are dihedral
transforms (rotations and reflections) of one photograph. That distinction is not cosmetic: it is
the same one Sect. 4.2 and Table 4 turn on, and it is why the ten-class Vietnamese dataset is
excluded from the comparison there. Transformed copies are a dataset-assembly property; separate
photographs of one specimen are a collection-process property.

**Why the standard check does not settle it.** `classify_redundancy.py` compares file bytes (MD5)
and decoded pixels. A 90° rotation has entirely different pixels under both, so it is scored as
*visually near but distinct* — that is, as capture-burst redundancy. The check below is therefore
run in addition to it, not instead of it.

**Method.** Each image is converted to greyscale and resized to 96 × 96. For every within-leaf
pair, one member is compared against all eight dihedral transforms of the other (four rotations ×
optional reflection), and the minimum mean absolute difference over the eight is recorded, on a
0–255 scale. A pair is classified as a transform duplicate at a threshold of 2.0. On synthetic
controls the separation is wide: an exact rotation returns 0.00, an unrelated image 85.5.

**Classes tested.** One uniform class, *Potato* healthy (152 images, 38 leaves, 4.00 per leaf), and
one non-uniform control, *Raspberry* healthy (371 images, 37 leaves, 10.03 per leaf).

| Class | Leaf groups | Within-leaf pairs | Median best-transform difference | p10 | p90 | Transform duplicates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Potato* healthy (4.00/leaf) | 38 | 228 | 29.65 | 23.60 | 36.12 | **0 (0.0%)** |
| *Raspberry* healthy (10.03/leaf) | 37 | 1,898 | 28.15 | 21.45 | 36.68 | **0 (0.0%)** |

**Result.** No pair among 2,126 is one image under any dihedral transform. The stronger evidence is
the second-order one: the two difference distributions are indistinguishable (medians 29.65 and
28.15, comparable spread), so the uniform class behaves exactly like the non-uniform control. A
mechanical duplication process would not produce that. The four images per leaf are four
photographs, and the covered subset is comparable with the session-level analysis rather than with
the duplicated-file case of Table 4.

**Scope.** The check covers rotation and reflection. It does not cover cropping or rescaling, so it
does not exclude the possibility that within-leaf images are different crops of one frame — though
a median difference near 30 on a 0–255 scale is difficult to reconcile with that. We state the
result at the scope tested.
